Global Temperature Change

How a warming planet is changing life in Malaysia

The Global Cause

Who Releases the Most Carbon Dioxide?

Carbon dioxide (CO2) is a gas that traps heat in the atmosphere and warms the whole planet. China and the United States alone release nearly half of the world's CO2, while Malaysia releases just 0.8%. However, Malaysia's hot climate means even small increases in temperature can have a noticeable impact.

What Produces Greenhouse Gases Worldwide?

Carbon dioxide traps heat in the atmosphere. Most emissions come from burning fuels burning fuels, with electricity and road transport responsible for nearly half of global emissions. This shows how much the world depends on coal, oil and gas.

Malaysia's Emissions Keep Rising

Although Malaysia contributes under 1% of the global total, its domestic emissions are expanding rapidly. Driven primarily by carbon dioxide from power plants and vehicles, yearly emissions have surged to nine times their 1970 level.

Malaysia Heats Up

Unlike temperate nations with four distinct seasons, Malaysia experiences steady tropical warmth year-round. Because there is no winter or cool period to provide relief, even an increase of 1°C significantly intensifies daily heat stress.

Malaysia Is Hot All Year

Malaysia's Recent Years Run Hotter Than Usual

Each line shows how much warmer or cooler a year was than Malaysia's 1991–2020 average. Since 2015, every year has been warmer than that average, and 2024 set a record at +0.90°C. Hot years are no longer rare exceptions; they have become normal.

Warming Across the Country

Weather Stations Across Malaysia

These weather stations supply the temperature data. They cover coastal and inland areas across Peninsular Malaysia, Sabah, and Sarawak, and most have between 30 and more than 70 years of records. That is long enough to reveal real long-term trends rather than short-term ups and downs.

Temperature by State

The slider above reflects state temperatures in 2025. Over the last seven decades, average temperatures have shifted upwards across the country, turning the map darker purple. In 2025, Pulau Pinang was the warmest state at 27.9°C.

Every State Is Warmer

Note: Each dot is a 30-year average. The earlier period (1951–1980) is compared with the most recent 30 years (1996–2025).

Comparing 1951–1980 with 1996–2025, every state and federal territory became warmer, by between 0.7°C and 1.3°C. Kuala Lumpur warmed the most (+1.3°C). One likely reason is the urban heat island effect: concrete, asphalt and buildings soak up heat during the day and release it at night, so cities stay warmer than the countryside around them.

What It Means

Malaysia's Rain Is Swinging Between Extremes

Each square is one month: brown means less rain than usual for that month, green means more. Since 2020, very wet months (dark green) appear more often. Warmer air holds more moisture, so when it rains, it rains harder, raising the risk of floods like those on the east coast in December 2014. El Niño is a periodic climate phenomenon that alters equatorial atmospheric circulation, bringing prolonged dry spells, reduced rainfall, and elevated temperatures across Southeast Asia. The 1998 event caused drought, haze and water shortages in Malaysia.

Two in Three Glaciers Could Vanish by Year 2100

Glaciers are slow-moving rivers of ice on high mountains and near the poles. If warming continues, about two-thirds of the world’s glaciers could disappear by 2100, raising sea levels. Despite having no glaciers, Malaysia faces risks to its low-lying coastal towns, ports and farms.

Heat-Related Illnesses During the Year 2016 Heatwave

During Malaysia’s 2016 heatwave, 200 people suffered heat-related illnesses, including 22 cases of life-threatening heat stroke. As extreme heat becomes more common, risks are expected to rise, especially for outdoor workers, older people and young children.
